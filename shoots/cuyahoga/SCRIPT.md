# The River That Burned — SCRIPT

**Facts (sources):**
- June 22, 1969, Cuyahoga River, Cleveland: a spark from a passing train ignited an oil slick floating against trestles near Republic Steel; fire lasted ~20–30 minutes; two railroad bridges damaged; no deaths/injuries; no photos of the 1969 fire exist (Time used file images). — National Park Service (nps.gov); EBSCO research-starters; AsTheySawIt account of the event.
- The Cuyahoga had caught fire at least 13 times; the 1952 fire was worse (~$1.3M damage, destroyed tugboat). Oil slick described as "yellowish-black rings ... like grease in soup" (Frantisek Vlcek memoir). — EBSCO; Wikipedia (via sources above).
- The 1969 fire became a national symbol and helped catalyze: first Earth Day (April 22, 1970), the EPA (Dec 1970), the Clean Water Act (1972); NEPA Jan 1970. Rivers/waters influence. — NPS; EBSCO; Outside Online (2019).
- 1998: designated an American Heritage River. March 20, 2019: fish from the river declared safe to eat by federal regulators. — Outside Online; NPS.
- Time magazine (Aug 1, 1969): river that "oozes rather than flows".

**Voice (steady tone):**
> In 1969, the Cuyahoga River caught fire... and nobody got a picture. Cleveland, Ohio. The summer of Woodstock and the moon landing — and a river, greased up by a century of dumped oil. A spark from a passing train, right above a trestle. Poof. Twenty-five minutes. Two railroad bridges scorched. No photo. No crew fast enough. Time magazine ran the story anyway, a month later: the river that oozes rather than flows.
> Luckily — the fire was small. Unluckily — it was the thirteenth.
> Oil floats. A spark does the rest. That is the whole trick — people had been pouring fuel into their own water for decades. Within a year: Earth Day. Within two: the Clean Water Act and a brand-new E.P.A.
> And the callback? March 2019 — fish caught in that same river were declared safe to eat. The river that refused to flow... now feeds people. Slow redemption.
