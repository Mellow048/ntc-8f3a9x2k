# The Building on Giant Springs

## Narration

This enormous building is sitting on what are basically giant shock absorbers. When the ground shakes, they let the building move differently from the earth underneath. This is San Francisco City Hall, a beautiful domed landmark in a city that knows earthquakes all too well. In nineteen eighty-nine, the Loma Prieta earthquake damaged the building. Engineers needed to protect it without throwing away its historic design. Their answer was to separate the building from its foundation with a system called base isolation. Imagine carrying a bowl of jelly on a tray while somebody shakes the table beneath you.

If the tray can slide a little, less of that shaking reaches the jelly. Under City Hall, special bearings made with layers of rubber and steel flex sideways during an earthquake. They help reduce the forces passed into the building above. A gap around the building gives it space to move without crashing into the surrounding ground. The bearings do not make an earthquake disappear, and the building still moves. But by letting the base shift in a controlled way, engineers gave this old landmark a much better chance of riding out the next big shake.

## Remaster fact and illustration notes

- Seismic retrofit contractor project description: https://www.olsonsteel.com/ability/san-francisco-city-hall/ — Loma Prieta damage; preserving the historic Beaux-Arts building; 530 lead-rubber isolators.
- City Hall virtual tour, renovation: https://sfch.budryerson.com/ — column-by-column retrofit, elastomeric bearings and controlled horizontal movement.
- The title's “giant springs” is an analogy, **not literal coil springs**. The picture shows layered rubber/steel bearings, their bolted end plates, and horizontal shear. The simplified cutaway represents the system, not an as-built plan or a literal count of isolators. Displacements are exaggerated for legibility; the building retains smaller, nonzero motion. No earthquake-proof guarantee is implied.
