In May, a man outside Lyon hired a digger to cut a hole for a swimming pool, and the bucket came up with plastic bags full of gold.
Five bars. Many coins. About seven hundred thousand euros, in the ground of a suburban garden.
He had bought the house not long before. And he did the thing almost nobody does: he rang the town hall.
In France a treasure is a thing buried so long that nobody can prove they own it, and found by chance. On your own land, the code says, it is yours.
Unless it is archaeology. Then it belongs to the state. So the culture ministry's regional office came to look.
It found no history in it at all: the gold had been melted fifteen, twenty years ago, by a company in Lyon.
The bars carry serial numbers. The gendarmes followed them, and found nothing stolen: the metal was bought legally, and never reported missing.
Then they went looking for the man who buried it. The previous owner of the house. He was dead.
No heir came forward. In November the prefecture said the gold was not heritage, and it went to the man with the pool.
Which leaves the tax. Finding it costs nothing. Selling it costs eleven and a half per cent, flat: eighty thousand five hundred euros. He keeps six hundred and nineteen thousand five hundred.
He has refused every interview. The local paper calls him the man who got rich digging a pool.
Somebody put seven hundred thousand euros in plastic bags in that garden, and never came back for it.
So who was he: a man hiding his savings from a bank, from a divorce, from the tax office, or from whoever came next?
