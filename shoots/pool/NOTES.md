# Notes: the gold under the pool

Reading notes, source by source, for `SCRIPT.md`. Read on 14 November 2026. Where two sources disagree the film says
so on screen, and `SCRIPT.md` records which figure was used and why.

## The find

- **BT** — The Brussels Times, 6 November 2025 (AFP). Neuville-sur-Saône, a town on the Saône north of Lyon. A
  homeowner, preparing the ground for a swimming pool, dug into buried gold in **May 2025**: five bars, "several
  hundred" coins in one account, "about forty" in another, in plastic bags. The town hall's estimate: **€700,000**. He
  reported it to the mairie, which referred it to the **DRAC** (direction régionale des affaires culturelles). The DRAC
  concluded the metal had **no archaeological interest**: it had been melted **15–20 years ago** by a company in the
  Lyon region.
- **RTL**, 6 November 2025. Same facts; adds that the gold was in "sacs plastique" and that the finder had bought the
  house recently. RTL's headline number is €700,000.
- **TF1 Info**, 6 November 2025. The bars are stamped; the melting was done by a professional firm; the purchase of the
  metal was legal and traceable.
- **WJ** — WJLA/ABC 7, November 2025. "Man keeps €700,000 in gold he found while digging pool." Frames the story around
  the finder keeping everything.

## The law

- **GR** — Greek Reporter, November 2025. Frames it under the **Civil Code, article 716**: treasure is anything hidden
  or buried of which no owner can be proved and which is discovered by chance; half to the finder, half to the owner of
  the land — and where the finder is the owner of the land, all of it to him. If the state classifies it as
  archaeological, the state's rules take over and the finder loses it.
- **BT / JP** — the reason this case is not article 716's archaeology case: the DRAC's "no archaeological interest"
  ruling removed the state's route to a claim.
- **GF** — Gold.fr, on buried treasure and tax. **Finding** a treasure is not a taxable event in France. **Selling**
  investment gold is taxed at a flat **11.5 %** (10.5 % plus 0.5 % CRDS) unless the seller elects the capital-gains
  regime, which needs a purchase price he does not have. On €700,000 that is **€80,500**.

## The owner before him

- **JP** — The Jerusalem Post, 8 November 2025. The **gendarmes** traced the serial numbers on the bars; nothing had
  been reported stolen; the metal had been bought legally. They then looked for the **previous owner of the house**,
  who is believed to have buried it. He had **died**, and **no heir came forward**.
- **CO** — ColombiaOne, November 2025. "No one came forward with a legal claim": the prefecture of the **Rhône**
  confirmed in November 2025 that the find could not be classified as archaeological remains, and the gold was returned
  to the finder.

## What the film leaves out, and why

- **The finder's name, face and age.** Not published. He has refused interviews. No likeness is drawn.
- **The exact count of coins.** Sources disagree (see above). The voice says "many coins"; the sticker says "41 GOLD
  COINS" with the town hall's €700,000, and the film never pretends the count is confirmed.
- **The identity of the dead owner, and why he buried it.** Unknown, and the film's last line is a question for
  exactly that reason: a bank, a divorce, the tax office, or "whoever came next" — the four ordinary reasons, offered
  as questions, with no answer asserted.
- **The neighbours' reaction, the town's name in the local paper's later stories.** Background colour, not needed.
- **The bar weights and the split between bars and coins.** Not published; the €700,000 is a single figure for the
  whole find and is presented as an estimate.
