In nineteen eleven, a handyman walks out of the Louvre with the Mona Lisa. For a whole day, nobody raises the alarm.
Vincenzo Peruggia once worked there. He helped build her glass case.
It's a Monday. The museum is closed. He says he just walks in, wearing a white smock, like the staff.
He lifts her off four hooks. In a stairwell, he strips off the frame and the glass.
Then, a locked door. He's unscrewing the doorknob when a plumber comes by, and kindly lets him out.
Next day, an artist comes to paint her, and finds four bare hooks. A guard shrugs. The photographers must have her.
They don't. The Louvre shuts for a week.
When it reopens, thousands line up to stare at an empty wall.
Police arrest a poet, and question a young Pablo Picasso.
They even question the handyman, at home. She's in a trunk, in the same room. They believe him.
Two years later, he tries to sell her to an art dealer in Florence. A patriot, he says, bringing home what Napoleon stole.
Napoleon never stole her. Leonardo brought her to France himself.
The dealer calls the police. The thief serves seven months.
She left a masterpiece that art lovers knew. She returns the most famous painting on Earth. It took an empty wall to make the world look.
