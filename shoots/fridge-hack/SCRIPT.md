**Status: in production.** News short about the cyberattack that heated supermarket freezers across France in October 2026, and France's accusation, on 9 November, that a hacking unit of Iran's Revolutionary Guard did it. Made in the order written in `references/news/README.md`, after the two films in `references/news/`.

### Could not be confirmed, and how the film handles it

- **Who did it.** France says it is certain. Iran denies it. Reuters could not establish how France reached its conclusion, and the minister would not say. The film never states it as a fact: "France says", "prosecutors cite", "Iran denies", each side in its own shot with its ministry's own English wording.
- **How France knows.** The prosecutor gives three points (row 14). The French agencies' report is said to list more technical indicators (TechSpot and ITdaily describe them; the report itself was not read for this film, and the details are the kind the last rule below keeps out). The minister would not say how the conclusion was reached (CNN), and the BBC wrote on 10 November that the French government "has not said how it reached the conclusion that Iran was responsible". The film gives the prosecutor's three points, then says that France will not say exactly how it is certain.
- **A rival claim.** On 4 November two pro-Russian hacktivist groups, NoName057(16) and Z-Pentest, said the freezer attacks were theirs. Their posts were deleted and nobody has verified the claim; the BBC wrote that "few believed them". It is not in the film, which blames the November attacks on nobody.
- **How the attackers got in.** Not published. In August researchers had shown a login bypass in one Danfoss controller model, since fixed, and counted thousands of controllers reachable from the internet; nobody has confirmed that this flaw was used. A claim that the login was "admin" and the date appears only on Wikipedia. Both left out. The film says what the prosecutor says: remote control through web interfaces.
- **How many sites and how many fires.** "About 700" from the prosecutor and the finance minister; early counts by the chains differ, and one headline says more than 1,000. Fires: "at least 15" in one official document, "roughly twenty" in another. The narration says "about seven hundred" and "more than a dozen".
- **How many warrants.** Reuters and AP: for the unit's leader and his two deputies. AFP and DW: for two officials. The film says "arrest warrants" and "for the unit's leaders", with no number.
- **How many countries in November.** AFP's count on the day: five, with more than 100 stores. Later reports name seven or more. The film says "from Sweden to Belgium" (Reuters' words) and, on screen, "at least five countries"; its board lights the five that every count has.
- **The cost.** One retailer's estimate, "tens of millions of euros". On screen as an estimate; not in the narration.
- **Whether the November attacks and the blood service incident are the same attackers.** Not established. The film says so in its own line.
- **The motive.** France calls it sabotage and gives no motive; analysts link it to the war. The narration gives no motive; one line of context on screen.
- **Freezers that failed on U.S. military bases in August.** Under investigation, no link established. Left out.
- **Single-source figures**: 80 °C in a store in Normandy, a setting of 110 °C at the blood service, "150 degrees" at a butcher's in Athens. Left out.
- **The three men named in the French report.** Accused, not tried. Not named and not drawn.
- **What IKEA lost.** IKEA said one store in Belgium was affected, nothing more. The joke says exactly that: no word on the meatballs.

### Rules for this film

- Who did it is always somebody's statement: "France says", "prosecutors cite". The denial gets its own shot and Iran's exact words.
- The two governments are treated alike: an anonymous spokesperson at a lectern in front of a landmark, beside the ministry's own English text. No caricature of either minister.
- Nobody accused is named or drawn. The hackers are hoods with no faces, the same in every shot, whoever they are.
- The November attacks are never blamed on anyone.
- Jokes are about freezers, defrosting, ice cream and meatballs. None about the war, about either country or its people, or about the blood service.
- Nothing that helps anyone repeat it: no model numbers, no login details, no steps. The control panel on screen is made up.
- Written for a viewer who has never heard of the story.

## Narration (235 words, 72.8 s as read)

1. Hackers turned up the heat in the freezers of about seven hundred French supermarkets. And France says Iran did it!
2. Overnight in October, some freezers hit fifty degrees Celsius. By morning: melted ice cream, spoiled meat, and more than a dozen fires.
3. Nobody set foot in a store. Someone got into the control panels online, muted the alarms, started a defrost, and never let it stop.
4. Why are freezers online? So technicians can watch them day and night.
5. One professor's verdict. Quote: a fridge doesn't look like a security risk, and that's exactly why attackers like it.
6. Danfoss, which makes many of those panels, points to outdated software.
7. Three weeks later, the same trick hit stores from Sweden to Belgium. Even an IKEA. No word on the meatballs.
8. Someone even went for freezers at France's blood service. Staff caught it in time.
9. Neither has been tied to the first attack.
10. For the first, France now names a culprit: a hacking unit of Iran's Revolutionary Guard.
11. Prosecutors cite its methods, traces of Farsi, and Iranian IP addresses. France says it's certain. How exactly? It won't say.
12. Iran denies it. And its reply is ice cold. Quote: a government that cannot even manage the temperature of its own refrigerators.
13. France wants arrest warrants and European sanctions.
14. The official advice? Take the freezers off the internet.
15. So what do you think: should a freezer be online at all?

- **The two spokespeople:** one at a lectern marked PARIS in front of the Eiffel Tower at dusk, one at a lectern marked TEHRAN in front of the Azadi Tower and snowy mountains. - Everybody else is anonymous: three shop workers, two shoppers, a firefighter, a service technician with a headset, a lab technician at the blood service, an investigator. A cat walks past the locked shop door.

Seen on the frames either side of every cut between two shots on the same set, and changed for the third render:

- Where the same thing is watched for several clauses, it is one take. - Where the thing changes and the place does not (the new panel and the old one), it is a match cut: the same `join`, so nothing but the panel moves.
- Otherwise two shots on the same set differ clearly in size: the two spokespeople and the sanctions folder got close shots.
- A stamp or name tag that used to vanish on such a cut now stays until the next graphic takes its place.
- Left as they were: the pairs where the whole picture changes anyway (the aisle hot and next morning, the shop front by day, on fire and locked, the blood service) and the evidence desk, where each shot is a different card.

- Level: the music sits **5.6 LU under the voice while someone is speaking, measured after ducking** (the standing benchmark in `IMPORTANT.md`: 5–6 LU was accepted, 9–11 LU was rejected as inaudible); 6.6 LU under above 300 Hz, which is all a phone speaker plays.
- Three things keep the words clear at that level: the music's 1.2–5 kHz band dips 5.5 dB under speech; no 200 ms of music may come closer to the voice than set ceilings, one for the speech band and one for the whole spectrum (a timpani roll is all bass, and the first ceiling alone did not see it); and accents were moved off the last syllables of quiet words onto cuts and gaps.
- Measured word by word against the approved GTA film with the same script: voice over everything else, median 5.2 LU, tenth percentile 1.8, worst single word −5.2 ("certain"); the GTA mix reads 5.3, 1.9 and −6.0.
- Compared with the other two news films: they are in A minor at 112 bpm and D minor at 104 bpm. This one shares neither key nor tempo, and neither of them has a second tune, brushes, a glockenspiel or a tape that slows down. It does share three instruments with the Nvidia film's score: a plucked bass, a vibraphone, and timpani with brass on the big words.
- Every change of shot but six carries a soft swish. Two of the swishes (frames 500 and 530) fall inside the control-panel take, where there is no cut: one goes with the pointer sliding to the defrost key, the other with the panel starting to shake. The mix file did not change when the cuts did.

The sound was set with meters only. The agent that made it did not listen to it.

One trap, fixed here and worth knowing: JavaScript's `Math.round` sends a half up and Python's `round()` sends it to the even number. Two words of this read start on exactly half a frame, and one of them starts a shot, so the shot list handed to the check was one frame off and the first render reported a jump that was only the cut. `build_mix.py` now rounds the way the picture does.
