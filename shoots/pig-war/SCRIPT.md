## Script (v2)

**I. The island** (0:00–0:42)

> One pig started a war between Britain and America. The pig lost. Nobody else got hurt.
>
> San Juan Island. Twenty miles long, seven wide, sitting between Vancouver Island and the mainland.
>
> In 1846 the two countries signed a border. It ran, they agreed, through the middle of the channel. There are two channels. Nobody wrote down which one.
>
> So for thirteen years, both countries owned the island on paper, and neither would move.

**II. The farm** (0:42–1:20)

> Britain got there first, with sheep. In 1853 the Hudson's Bay Company landed thirteen hundred sheep on the south end and called the place Belle Vue. Six years later there were four thousand, plus cattle, horses, and forty hogs that went wherever they liked.
>
> Americans came anyway. Eighteen, twenty-five of them, gold miners who had missed the gold rush and wanted land. They cut claims out of the Company's best pasture.
>
> Washington Territory sent the Company a tax bill: eighty dollars and thirty-three cents. The Company refused to pay, and the county threatened to auction the sheep.
>
> Nobody fired a shot in six years of that. Then a pig got into a potato patch.

**III. The pig** (1:20–1:52)

> June fifteenth, 1859. Lyman Cutlar, twenty-something, from Kentucky, looked out of his cabin at a black boar in his potatoes. It was not the first time. He had already asked Griffin to keep his hogs off the land. This time he had had enough.
>
> The pig belonged to Charles Griffin, who ran Belle Vue for the Company. Griffin wanted a hundred dollars. Cutlar offered ten, and said there was a better chance of lightning striking Griffin than of him getting a hundred dollars for that hog.
>
> Britain threatened to arrest him and put every American off the island. So the settlers did the obvious thing. They asked their army to come.

**IV. The armies** (1:52–2:30)

> General William Harney sent Captain George Pickett with sixty-four men. Pickett camped a mile north of the farm and declared the island American.
>
> Britain sent five warships. By August there were four hundred and sixty-one Americans behind an earthwork with fourteen guns, facing two thousand British sailors and marines and a hundred and sixty-seven guns. On an island with more sheep than people.
>
> Both sides had the same orders. Defend yourself. Do not fire first.
>
> Then the governor of Vancouver Island ordered the marines ashore, and Admiral Baynes read the order and refused. Two great nations, he said, in a war over a squabble about a pig.
>
> Victoria ran excursion boats out to watch. Officers from the two camps went to church together on a British warship, and drank whisky in Griffin's front room.

**V. Twelve years** (2:30–3:00)

> Washington was horrified. President Buchanan sent General Winfield Scott, who talked both sides into a joint occupation: one company each, a hundred men, until the diplomats settled it. Harney was recalled.
>
> The diplomats took twelve years. The marines built English Camp on Garrison Bay: barracks, a hospital, a blockhouse, tennis courts, a bowling green, a library, a billiard room, and a garden. On the hill above, a small cemetery. The Americans held the windy south end, and waited.
>
> In 1861 Pickett went home to join the Confederacy. Two years later he led the charge at Gettysburg. The island he had claimed was still waiting.

**VI. The line** (3:00–3:20)

> In 1871 the two countries finally agreed to arbitrate, and handed the question to the Kaiser of Germany. A commission argued in Geneva for a year. On the twenty-first of October, 1872, the Kaiser drew the line through Haro Strait.
>
> The island was American. Six weeks later the marines marched down to their boats.

**Coda** (3:20–3:45)

> Lyman Cutlar never owned the land he shot the pig for. He left the island, and died in 1874, aged forty. Charles Griffin stayed. The American settlers elected him their constable.
>
> Nobody died in the Pig War except the pig. The camps are a national park now, and the garden at English Camp is planted every spring.
>
> The war lasted twelve years. The garden is still going.
